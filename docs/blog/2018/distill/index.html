Introduction: Breaking AlphaFold2

The field of structural biology has been revolutionized by AlphaFold2, but proteins aren't static structures—they're dynamic ensembles. This realization has driven me to explore BioEMU, Microsoft's approach to generating protein conformational ensembles using molecular dynamics simulations. This post synthesizes my research notes, talk insights with a senior researcher from Microsoft's BioEMU team, and the broader implications for computational biology.

We circled back to a fundamental truth: proteins are not frozen sculptures but rather dynamic entities that breathe, flex, and transition between multiple conformational states. This dynamism is not a bug—it's a feature that enables biological function. Enzymes need conformational changes for catalysis, receptors must shift shape upon ligand binding, and antibodies require flexibility to recognize diverse antigens.

The Core Problem: What AlphaFold2 Misses AlphaFold2 excels at predicting single static structures but fails to capture:
  • Cryptic pockets - Hidden binding sites that appear only in certain conformations, critical for drug discovery
  • Kinase dynamics - Conformational changes essential for activation/deactivation mechanisms
  • Dynamic ensembles - The full repertoire of protein conformational states and their populations
  • RAS proteins - Notoriously flexible proteins with multiple functional states, important cancer targets
  • Intrinsically disordered proteins - Proteins that lack fixed 3D structure but are functionally important
  • Allosteric mechanisms - Long-range conformational communication between distant sites
  • Induced fit - How proteins adjust their shape upon ligand or partner binding
Current State of the Field While AlphaFold2 achieves near-experimental accuracy for static structure prediction, functional understanding requires conformational dynamics. Proteins function through motion—catalysis, binding, and regulation all depend on accessing multiple conformational states. The challenge is that experimental techniques for capturing dynamics are expensive and time-consuming: NMR provides ensemble information but at limited resolution, cryo-EM can capture multiple states but requires extensive data processing, and molecular dynamics simulations are computationally expensive and limited by force field accuracy.

Critical Questions Facing the Field:
  • How do we validate ensemble predictions when experiments often capture only dominant states?
  • What's the minimal ensemble size needed for different applications (drug design vs. mechanistic understanding)?
  • Can we predict transition pathways between conformational states, not just the endpoints?
  • How do we balance computational cost with ensemble completeness?
  • What confidence can we assign to rare conformational states predicted by models?
Path Forward: The field is converging toward hybrid approaches that combine evolutionary information (MSAs), physics simulations (MD), and machine learning (transformers/diffusion models) to generate physically plausible, functionally relevant conformational ensembles. BioEMU represents one of the most sophisticated implementations of this vision, training diffusion models on extensive MD simulation data while maintaining the evolutionary signal from AlphaFold2's Evoformer.

BioEMU Architecture: A Two-Stage Training Approach

BioEMU represents a significant advancement in protein structure prediction by explicitly incorporating conformational dynamics. The architecture builds upon AlphaFold2's Evoformer but extends it with molecular dynamics simulation data through a carefully designed two-stage training process.

Stage 1: Pre-training with AlphaFold2 Evoformer

The first stage uses the AlphaFold2 Evoformer module completely unchanged. This is critical—BioEMU doesn't reinvent the MSA-based structure prediction wheel. This architectural decision was emphasized as strategic: the Evoformer has been refined through extensive research and captures evolutionary constraints exceptionally well. Rather than competing with this component, BioEMU builds on top of it.

This decision has profound implications. By inheriting the Evoformer, BioEMU automatically benefits from any improvements made to AlphaFold2's MSA processing. However, it also inherits the limitations—the computational cost of MSA generation and processing, the requirement for evolutionary coverage, and the bottleneck in inference speed.

Critical Considerations: Architectural Inheritance
  • Evolutionary information capture: The Evoformer excels at extracting co-evolutionary signals from MSAs that encode structural constraints. This information is crucial for predicting not just the dominant conformation but also the conformational landscape.
  • Transfer learning advantage: Pre-trained AlphaFold2 weights can potentially be leveraged, dramatically reducing training time and data requirements. The team doesn't need to re-learn protein structure prediction from scratch.
  • Modularity tradeoff: While this separation is architecturally clean, it means improvements to sequence/MSA processing won't automatically benefit from ensemble training signals. The gradient doesn't flow from the dynamics layer back to improve MSA processing.
  • Scalability implications: The Evoformer remains the computational bottleneck for inference. Each ensemble prediction still requires running the expensive MSA processing pipeline, limiting throughput for large-scale applications.
  • Dependency risk: BioEMU's performance is bounded by the Evoformer's capabilities. If AlphaFold2 fails on a target (e.g., due to poor MSA quality), BioEMU will likely struggle as well.

Stage 2: Diffusion Model Fine-tuning with MD Data

The second stage is where BioEMU diverges dramatically from traditional structure prediction. This is where the magic happens—the model learns to generate conformational ensembles rather than single structures. The training data is extraordinary in scale and quality:

  • ~200 milliseconds of molecular dynamics simulation data per protein—to put this in perspective, this represents 2000-20000x more simulation time than typical academic lab runs of 10-100 nanoseconds. This massive simulation data provides ground truth for what conformational space proteins actually explore.
  • A diffusion model architecture for generating ensemble conformations—similar to image generation models but operating in 3D coordinate space with physical constraints.
  • Training on approximately 300,000 proteins without ligands, coenzymes, or those that are membrane-based—a curated set designed to focus on pure protein dynamics without complicating factors.
  • Free energy prediction accuracy of ~1 kcal/mol—approaching chemical accuracy, which is the threshold for many biological processes.
Technical Implementation: The Distributed Graph Former Approach
  • Graph-based transformer architecture: Proteins are represented as graphs where residues are nodes and interactions are edges. This representation is more natural for protein structure than treating them as sequences or images.
  • GPU + Transformer model (6NU architecture): The model combines transformer attention mechanisms with geometric neural networks, allowing it to capture both sequence patterns and spatial relationships.
  • Diffusion model for sampling: The model learns to denoise structures, starting from random coordinates and gradually refining them into physically plausible conformations. This process can be run multiple times to generate an ensemble.
  • Boltzmann distribution matching: The model is trained to generate conformations with populations matching the Boltzmann distribution from the MD simulations, ensuring thermodynamic validity.
  • Electrostatics modeling: Proper treatment of electrostatic interactions is crucial for conformational dynamics, particularly for charged residues and salt bridges that stabilize or destabilize certain states.

The Molecular Dynamics Foundation

Understanding BioEMU requires appreciating the computational chemistry foundation it's built on. Molecular dynamics simulations solve Newton's equations of motion for every atom in a protein, calculating forces and updating positions thousands or millions of times to simulate protein motion. This physics-based approach provides the ground truth that BioEMU learns from.

Computational Chemistry: From Quantum Mechanics to Classical Dynamics The hierarchy of simulation methods represents fundamental tradeoffs between accuracy and scale:
  • Quantum Mechanics (QM): Founded by Heisenberg and Schrödinger, QM provides the most accurate description of atomic interactions. However, it's computationally tractable only for systems up to ~100 atoms with high accuracy.
  • Classical Molecular Dynamics: Developed in the late 1970s, MD uses polynomial approximations to QM, allowing simulation of systems with thousands or millions of atoms. The tradeoff is reduced accuracy, particularly for electronic effects like charge transfer or bond breaking.
  • Force Fields: Classical MD relies on force fields—mathematical functions that approximate atomic interactions. Traditional force fields (AMBER, CHARMM) use fixed parameters, while modern approaches are incorporating machine learning.
  • Neural Network Potentials: These represent the cutting edge—training neural networks on QM calculations to achieve near-QM accuracy at classical MD speed. The accuracy is closer to QM for energy and force calculations while being much faster than ab initio QM.
  • Schrödinger Force Fields: Commercial implementations that use sophisticated physics-based models, achieving better accuracy than traditional force fields for certain systems.
The Central Question: Should we focus on making MD more accurate (better force fields, neural network potentials) or on learning directly from imperfect MD data with sophisticated models like BioEMU? The answer is probably both—better physics improves what models can learn, and better models can correct for physics approximations.

From Data Scarcity to Structure-Guided Design

My path to this discussion reflects the broader challenges facing computational biology: how do we make predictions when data is scarce, biased, or incomplete? My work on MHC-peptide binding prediction embodies this struggle and the creative solutions required.

Project 1: Transformer-Based MHC Binding Prediction

My Research Approach: Adapting NLP to Biology I described my work developing transformer-based models for MHC-peptide binding prediction, addressing the field's core challenge of data scarcity. The MHC system presents a perfect storm of data challenges: thousands of different MHC alleles, but many with fewer than 10 measured binding peptides. The available datasets are also systematically biased—mass spectrometry inherently samples peptides that actually bind to MHC, creating a distribution skewed toward binders with very few non-binder examples.

To address this, I've adapted strategies from natural language processing, specifically continued domain pre-training. The approach has two stages: first, continue the pre-training process on MHC-bound epitopes (unlabeled data) to learn domain-specific patterns. Second, fine-tune on binding affinity measurements (labeled data) paired with peptide sequences. This allows the model to learn general peptide-MHC interaction patterns from abundant unlabeled data, then specialize to predict binding affinity with limited labeled examples.

The inspiration from NLP is direct: just as language models benefit from pre-training on unlabeled text before fine-tuning on specific tasks, protein models can benefit from domain-specific pre-training. The key insight is that there's far more unlabeled data (peptide sequences known to bind MHC, but without quantified affinity) than labeled data (peptides with measured IC50 values).

Critical Considerations: Low-Data Regime Strategies
  • Data scarcity mitigation through continued pre-training: When labeled data is limited but unlabeled domain data exists, continued pre-training is essential. For MHC, we have thousands of peptides known to bind (from mass spec) but only hundreds with quantified binding affinity.
  • Addressing systematic bias: Mass spectrometry samples peptides presented on cell surfaces—these are all binders by definition. This creates severe label imbalance: many positive examples, very few negative examples. Traditional supervised learning struggles with this distribution. We need to either synthesize negative examples carefully or use techniques like positive-unlabeled learning.
  • Geometry as regularization prior: Structure-based constraints can regularize sequence generation in low-data regimes. If we know the MHC binding groove structure, we can constrain predictions to be geometrically plausible even when data is sparse.
  • Complementary approaches for different failure modes: Data-driven methods (transformers) excel when patterns exist in training data but fail for out-of-distribution examples. Physics-based methods (geometric constraints) generalize better but may miss subtle patterns. Combining both addresses different failure modes.
  • Transfer learning from NLP surprisingly effective: Strategies developed for language (continued pre-training, domain adaptation, few-shot learning) transfer remarkably well to protein sequences. This suggests proteins and language share some fundamental statistical properties.

Project 2: Structure-Guided Peptide Generation with RF Diffusion

Escaping Data Bias Through Geometry My second project takes an orthogonal approach: rather than predicting binding for existing peptides, generate novel peptides that are geometrically compatible with MHC binding grooves. Using RF diffusion and ProteinMPNN, we can explore sequence space that's out-of-distribution for data-hungry predictive models, while maintaining geometric compatibility with MHC structures. This approach sidesteps the biases present in mass spectrometry data by using geometric constraints from the MHC binding groove structure itself.

The key insight is that we can constrain the generation process based on the geometry of the MHC binding groove. Certain positions (P2 and P9 anchor positions) have well-defined pockets that accommodate specific amino acid types. By using these geometric constraints during generation, we can create peptides that should bind even if they've never been seen in training data.

This project represents a fundamentally different philosophy: rather than learning from biased data, use physics and geometry to guide generation. RF diffusion learns the manifold of physically plausible protein structures, and by conditioning on the MHC scaffold, we can generate peptides that fit geometrically. ProteinMPNN then designs sequences that fold into these structures.

The Data Scarcity Challenge in MHC Research MHC-peptide binding prediction faces extreme data imbalance. Consider the numbers: there are over 20,000 characterized MHC alleles in the human population. For the most common alleles (like HLA-A*02:01), we might have 10,000+ measured peptides. For rare alleles, we have fewer than 10. Traditional supervised learning requires hundreds or thousands of examples per class—we simply don't have this for most alleles.

Additional Challenge—Distribution Bias: The data we do have is systematically biased. Mass spectrometry samples peptides actually presented on cell surfaces—these are all successful binders. We have very few examples of non-binders, making it hard to learn what makes a good binder versus a bad one. It's like trying to learn what makes a good restaurant by only studying Michelin-starred establishments—you know they're good, but you don't know what makes a bad restaurant bad.

My Two-Pronged Solution:
  1. Continued pre-training (data-driven): Leverage unlabeled peptide sequences to learn general MHC-peptide patterns, then fine-tune on limited labeled data for specific alleles.
  2. Structure-guided generation (physics-driven): Use geometric constraints from MHC structures to generate peptides that are geometrically compatible, even for alleles with no training data.
When to Use Each:
  • Data-rich alleles (>1000 examples): Pure transformer approach works well
  • Data-poor alleles (<10 examples): Structure-guided generation is more reliable
  • Intermediate alleles (10-1000 examples): Hybrid approach using structure to regularize transformer predictions
This creates a spectrum of methods matched to data availability, rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all solution.

BioEMU and my intersets

My Motivation: The Underappreciated Revolution I explained my deep dive into BioEMU and its somewhat underappreciated position in the field. While AlphaFold and RF diffusion dominate academic Twitter and conference discussions, BioEMU represents a crucial stepping stone that doesn't get enough attention. The fundamental insight—that proteins exist in conformational ensembles rather than static states—addresses a core limitation of AlphaFold2.

Understanding how proteins transition between states, from globular closed conformations to open extended states, directly addresses known failures of static structure prediction. Kinases are a perfect example: AlphaFold2 often predicts inactive conformations because those are most common in the PDB, but understanding kinase function requires knowing both active and inactive states plus the transition between them.

The clinical implications are profound. My work on MHC-peptide binding is fundamentally limited by treating the MHC-peptide complex as static. But the binding groove has measurable flexibility—it can adjust shape to accommodate different peptides. This induced-fit mechanism affects not just which peptides bind, but how they're recognized by T-cell receptors. A conformationally-aware model could dramatically improve vaccine design by predicting not just binding, but immunogenicity.

The Interpretability Vision: Understanding What Models Learn

My Interpretability Goals: Opening the Black Box I emphasized my strong interest in applying mechanistic interpretability techniques to BioEMU. Using methods like linear probing and sparse autoencoders, we could systematically dissect what features make BioEMU effective at generating ensembles. While the current generation of models is impressive in predictive performance, there's surprisingly little interpretability work in structural biology—much less than in NLP or computer vision.

The motivation for interpretability is multifaceted. First, understanding what BioEMU learns about conformational dynamics could reveal principles we can apply to other systems. Second, interpretability enables debugging—when the model fails, we can diagnose why rather than just collecting more data blindly. Third, clinical applications demand interpretability for regulatory approval and clinician trust. Fourth, mechanistic understanding enables targeted improvements rather than blind architecture search.

Interpretability Toolkit for Structural Biology
  • Linear probing: Train simple linear classifiers on frozen model representations to predict physical properties. If a linear probe can predict B-factors (atomic flexibility) from BioEMU representations, this suggests the model explicitly encodes flexibility information. This technique has been extremely successful in NLP for understanding what BERT layers encode.
  • Sparse Autoencoders (SAE): Decompose dense neural representations into interpretable, sparse features. The key insight from NLP: neurons in transformers are polysemantic (activate for many unrelated concepts), but sparse linear combinations of neurons can be monosemantic (represent single interpretable concepts). SAEs have successfully been applied to ESM-2, revealing features corresponding to secondary structure, burial, and functional sites.
  • Feature attribution and saliency: Understanding which input features (residues, sequence positions, MSA patterns) drive specific conformational predictions. This could reveal, for example, which residues act as conformational switches.
  • Attention pattern analysis: Examining what the model attends to during ensemble generation. In AlphaFold2, attention patterns were found to correspond to structural features like contacts and secondary structure. What do BioEMU's attention patterns reveal about conformational dynamics?
  • Causal interventions: Systematically modifying representations and observing effects on predictions. This goes beyond correlation to test causal relationships: does increasing the "flexibility" feature in the representation actually lead to more diverse ensembles?
The Gap in Structural Biology: Most ML research in structural biology focuses on performance metrics—accuracy, coverage, speed. Far less work examines what models learn or how they fail. This is partly because benchmarks in biology are limited (unlike vision with ImageNet or NLP with GLUE), making it hard to systematically probe model behavior. But it's also cultural—biology has strong traditions of mechanistic understanding, but ML-biology work often treats models as black boxes.
Current State: The Interpretability Deficit Despite the success of ML in structural biology, we have limited mechanistic understanding of what these models learn. We know AlphaFold2 works incredibly well, but we don't fully understand how it extracts structural information from MSAs. We know ESM-2 captures something about protein structure from sequence alone, but what exactly? We know BioEMU generates conformational ensembles, but does it understand thermodynamics, or is it doing something else?

Critical Questions for BioEMU Interpretability:
  • Do BioEMU representations encode thermodynamic quantities like free energy barriers between conformational states?
  • Are conformational dynamics represented explicitly (dedicated features for flexibility, hinge regions), or do they emerge implicitly from structural features?
  • Which parts of the model learn physics (force fields, energetics) versus statistical correlations (frequent patterns in training data)?
  • Can we identify when the model is uncertain versus confident in its ensemble predictions?
  • Does the model understand kinetics (transition rates) or just thermodynamics (state populations)?
  • Can interpretability reveal why certain protein families are harder to model than others?
Optimal Path Forward: Systematic Interpretability Research
  1. Physical property probing: Train linear classifiers to predict B-factors, flexibility metrics, conformational switching propensity, binding pocket presence—all from BioEMU representations. Success indicates explicit encoding of these properties.
  2. Sparse feature identification: Apply SAEs to identify interpretable feature dimensions. Do we find features corresponding to hinge regions, flexible loops, rigid cores, allosteric pathways?
  3. Causal intervention experiments: Modify representations and observe effects. If we amplify a "flexibility" feature, do we get more diverse ensembles? If we suppress a "stability" feature, do we get larger conformational changes?
  4. Validation against ground truth: Compare learned features to properties computed from MD simulations. Do BioEMU representations capture what MD simulations show about conformational dynamics?
  5. Cross-protein family analysis: How do representations differ between rigid proteins (small globular) and flexible proteins (multi-domain, intrinsically disordered)? This reveals what the model has learned about different types of dynamics.
Expected Impact: This wouldn't just improve BioEMU—it would establish interpretability methodology for structural biology ML, build community trust through mechanistic understanding, and enable principled model improvement rather than blind architecture search.

Critical Questions & Open Challenges

The Two-Conformation Sampling Puzzle

One aspect of BioEMU that particularly intrigued me during my research was the decision to average just two structures sampled from the final conformational distribution. This seems conservative given that the model learns a full distribution over conformational space.

My Observation and Analysis BioEMU's approach of averaging two structures sampled from the conformational distribution seemed conservative during my analysis of the architecture. While this likely reduces noise from the stochastic diffusion process, it raises fundamental questions: why limit sampling to just two conformations when we have access to the full learned distribution? Why not sample more extensively to better capture conformational diversity?

This choice appears in tension with the goal of ensemble modeling. If we're trying to capture the conformational landscape, shouldn't we sample it more thoroughly? Or is there something optimal about two samples that balances information content with practical usability?

Several possible explanations emerged from my analysis and the discussion:

  • Computational efficiency for production deployment: Generating each sample requires running the diffusion process, which is computationally expensive. For a web service or high-throughput application, limiting to two samples makes deployment feasible.
  • Noise reduction through averaging: The diffusion process is stochastic—each sample has some random noise. Averaging two samples reduces this noise while staying computationally reasonable. More samples would reduce noise further, but with diminishing returns.
  • Uncertainty quantification heuristic: Two samples provide a minimal estimate of conformational variability without overwhelming users. The distance between the two samples indicates how much the protein moves.
  • Validation against experimental standards: Many experimental ensemble techniques (NMR, cryo-EM) report 2-5 representative conformations. Matching this convention makes BioEMU predictions comparable to experimental data.
  • User experience and interpretability: Presenting 10-50 conformations to biologists creates cognitive overload. Two structures provide clear intuition: "closed" versus "open," "active" versus "inactive." This interpretability is crucial for adoption.
  • Training data constraints: If the MD simulations used for training typically extracted 2-3 representative structures, the model may be optimized for this regime.
Current Challenges in Ensemble Size Determination
  • No consensus on optimal size: Different applications need different ensemble sizes. Drug discovery might benefit from 50+ conformations to find cryptic pockets, while mechanistic biology might only need 2-3 representative states.
  • Computational cost scales linearly: Each additional sample requires another diffusion process run. This adds up quickly for large-scale predictions.
  • Experimental validation typically captures 2-5 states: NMR ensembles are often 10-20 structures, but only 2-5 are truly distinct. Cryo-EM classification usually finds 2-5 major classes. This experimental reality shapes expectations.
  • Downstream tools struggle with large ensembles: Molecular docking software, MD simulation setup, and visualization tools are often designed for single structures. Handling 50 conformations requires workflow redesign.
  • Cognitive limitations: Humans struggle to conceptualize and compare more than ~5 objects simultaneously. Presenting 10+ conformations to experimentalists creates analysis paralysis.
  • Validation becomes harder: How do we validate that 50 predicted conformations are correct? Generating 50 experimental structures is rarely feasible.
Current Practice vs. Potential Most ensemble-aware experimental techniques report 2-5 representative conformations as "the ensemble." NMR might generate 20 structures, but these are often averaged or clustered to 3-5 representatives. Cryo-EM might collect thousands of particle images, but classification typically reveals 2-5 major conformational classes. BioEMU's two-sample approach matches this experimental convention, making predictions directly comparable.

However, this potentially underutilizes the model's capacity. BioEMU learns a full distribution—why collapse it to just two samples? The model knows about rare conformational states, transition pathways, and the full conformational landscape. Much of this information is lost when we only sample twice.

Key Tradeoff to Consider:
  • More samples → Better coverage: Improved discovery of cryptic pockets, better representation of conformational diversity, more accurate ensemble averages
  • More samples → More problems: Higher computational cost, harder validation, reduced usability, difficult visualization and interpretation
Optimal Solution: Application-Dependent Adaptive Sampling Rather than fixed two-sample approach, future versions could provide adaptive sampling:
  • Drug discovery mode (extensive sampling): Generate 10-50 conformations to comprehensively search for cryptic pockets and transient binding sites. Computational cost is justified by pharmaceutical value.
  • Clinical diagnostics mode (minimal sampling): Two representative states (e.g., active/inactive for kinases) for clear interpretation. Reliability and interpretability trump comprehensiveness.
  • Protein engineering mode (distribution sampling): Sample the full conformational distribution to understand the design space and predict effects of mutations on conformational landscape.
  • Mechanistic studies mode (transition path sampling): Focus sampling on the pathway between known conformational states to understand mechanism of conformational change.
  • Uncertainty-weighted sampling: Use more samples when the model is uncertain (high conformational diversity), fewer when confident (rigid protein). This adapts computational cost to difficulty.
Future Implementation: BioEMU 2.0 could provide a confidence-weighted sampling parameter where users specify desired confidence level, and the model determines how many samples are needed to achieve it. Low-confidence regions (flexible loops, disordered regions) need more samples; high-confidence regions (rigid cores) need fewer.

Data Sources for Next-Generation Models

What are the next sources of information?

My Initial Response: MHC and Cryo-EM I initially focused on MHC complexes, highlighting their well-characterized binding grooves and the wealth of structural data across alleles. The P2 and P9 anchor positions are particularly well-studied—these positions in bound peptides have strong preferences based on the specific pockets in the MHC groove. I've personally curated a dataset of 100-200 MHC structures spanning different alleles, which already show conformational variation in existing crystal structures without requiring new simulations.

I also mentioned emerging cryo-EM work from labs at Berkeley. Cryo-EM has the advantage of capturing conformational heterogeneity natively—particles in different conformational states are all present in the data, and advanced classification methods can separate them. However, the data is noisier than crystallography and requires sophisticated 3D reconstruction algorithms. The resolution is also generally lower, though this is rapidly improving with better detectors and software.

Many MHC structures are already in the PDB and thus included in AlphaFold2's training data. This pushed me to think more broadly about truly untapped data sources that aren't just "more of the same."

Critical Considerations: Beyond Static Structures
  • PDB saturation reality: The ~200,000 structures in PDB have been exhaustively mined by every major structure prediction method. Incremental additions (a few thousand new structures per year) provide diminishing returns for model improvement.
  • Cryo-EM's unique advantages: Unlike crystallography, which selects for stable conformations, cryo-EM captures proteins in solution with their natural conformational heterogeneity. 3D classification can separate conformational states directly from particle images.
  • Cryo-EM's challenges: Lower resolution than crystallography (3-4Å typical vs. 2Å for crystals), requires massive computational resources for 3D reconstruction, class averaging may obscure rare conformational states that are biologically important.
  • The "dark matter" of structural biology: Industrial labs (pharmaceutical companies) and well-funded academic labs hold vast amounts of unpublished structural data—failed experiments, intermediate structures, incomplete datasets. This data could be valuable for training but is rarely shared.
  • Functional vs. static data: We need data that captures proteins in action, not just frozen snapshots. Time-resolved techniques, perturbation experiments, and functional assays provide information orthogonal to static structures.
  • Multi-modal integration challenges: Combining structural data (high resolution, static) with dynamics data (lower resolution, time-resolved) and functional data (no structure, just behavior) requires new training paradigms. Models must learn to integrate heterogeneous information sources.
Current State: The Data Plateau We've exhausted static structural databases for pre-training. AlphaFold2 was trained on essentially all available protein structures. Adding 5,000 new structures won't dramatically improve the model—we've already learned the general principles of protein structure. Most modern structure prediction models are now data-limited rather than model-limited: architectural improvements provide small gains compared to what more/better data could provide.

Cryo-EM offers conformational heterogeneity, but requires new analysis paradigms. Traditional cryo-EM processing averages particles to get high resolution, throwing away conformational diversity. Modern techniques like 3DVA (3D variational analysis) and cryoDRGN use deep learning to capture continuous conformational variation, but adoption is still limited.

Untapped Data Sources to Consider:
  • Time-resolved crystallography: Uses laser pulses to trigger protein conformational changes, then captures structures at different time points. Provides snapshots of proteins in action, revealing transition states and intermediate conformations.
  • HDX-MS (Hydrogen-Deuterium Exchange Mass Spectrometry): Reports on conformational dynamics and flexibility by measuring which parts of the protein are accessible to solvent exchange. Provides residue-level flexibility information for entire proteins in solution.
  • FRET (Förster Resonance Energy Transfer): Measures distance distributions between labeled sites on proteins in solution. Provides ensemble-averaged distance information that could constrain conformational distributions.
  • NMR ensemble data: NMR directly reports on conformational heterogeneity through peak broadening, chemical shift changes, and relaxation rates. While NMR structures have lower resolution than crystallography, they capture solution-state dynamics.
  • Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS): Provides low-resolution solution-state envelope information. While not atomic resolution, SAXS is exquisitely sensitive to conformational changes and can validate ensemble predictions.
  • Metagenomic sequences from uncultured organisms: The vast majority of microbial diversity is uncultured. Metagenomic sequencing reveals proteins from extreme environments (thermophiles, psychrophiles, acidophiles) that explore different regions of protein sequence space.
  • Deep mutational scanning data: Systematic mutagenesis experiments that measure fitness effects of all possible mutations. Provides functional information that could constrain structure predictions.
  • Industrial high-throughput screening data: Pharmaceutical companies generate massive amounts of binding, stability, and expression data. While proprietary, federated learning could enable training on this data without sharing it.
  • Computational mutagenesis at scale: In silico perturbation experiments—predict effects of mutations, then validate experimentally. This active learning loop could efficiently explore sequence space.
Optimal Integration Strategy for Multi-Modal Data:
  1. Multi-modal pre-training: Train models that can process multiple data types—structural data (PDB, cryo-EM), dynamics data (HDX-MS, NMR), evolutionary data (MSAs), and functional data (binding assays). Use contrastive learning to align representations across modalities.
  2. Weak supervision frameworks: Use noisy experimental signals (SAXS, FRET, HDX-MS) as soft constraints rather than hard targets. The model learns to approximately satisfy these constraints while maintaining physical plausibility.
  3. Transfer from physics simulations: Leverage MD simulations from different force fields (AMBER, CHARMM, etc.) as complementary views of the same system. Disagreement between force fields indicates uncertainty; agreement indicates confidence.
  4. Active learning for targeted experiments: Use model uncertainty to identify proteins where experimental characterization would maximally improve predictions. Focus limited experimental resources on high-value targets.
  5. Federated learning for proprietary data: Enable pharmaceutical companies and industrial labs to contribute to model training without sharing proprietary data. Models are trained locally, only gradient updates are shared.
  6. Synthetic data augmentation: Use generative models to create synthetic training examples that are physically plausible but not in the training set. This can help models generalize beyond observed data.
The Future: Next-generation models will be inherently multi-modal, integrating static structures with dynamics information, evolutionary constraints with functional data, and experimental observations with simulation predictions. The challenge is developing training frameworks that can learn from this heterogeneous data in a principled way.

The MSA Debate: Evolutionary Information vs. Single Sequence

One of the most fundamental debates in current structure prediction is whether multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) are truly necessary, or whether single-sequence models augmented with language model embeddings can match their performance.

Discussion on MSAs and the SimpleFold Failure I raised the tension between MSA-based approaches and single-sequence models, especially given the push toward synthetic data generation from language models. The perspective was unambiguous: MSAs remain essential. While some researchers argue that single-sequence approaches augmented with language models can match MSA-based performance, empirical evidence suggests otherwise.

The Apple SimpleFold paper served as a cautionary tale. They attempted to replace AlphaFold2's MSA pathway with embeddings from the ESM language model—essentially asking whether ESM could capture everything that MSAs provide. The result was clear: SimpleFold achieved worse performance than AlphaFold2 across benchmarks. This demonstrates that evolutionary information from MSAs cannot yet be fully replaced by language models, despite ESM's impressive capabilities.

This failure is informative. ESM-2 was trained on 65 million protein sequences and captures remarkable structural information from sequence alone. It can predict secondary structure, solvent accessibility, and even contact maps without explicit evolutionary input. Yet it still cannot fully replace MSAs for structure prediction. What is MSAs capturing that language models miss?

What MSAs Provide That Language Models Don't
  • Co-evolutionary constraints: MSAs reveal which positions co-vary—when position i mutates, position j often mutates to compensate. This co-evolution reflects structural contacts and functional constraints that shape the protein's fold. Language models see individual sequences but not these correlated mutation patterns.
  • Pairwise relationship information: The Evoformer processes MSAs to create pair representations—information about relationships between residue pairs. This is crucial for predicting contacts and ultimately structure. Single-sequence models lack this explicit pairwise pathway.
  • Family-specific patterns: MSAs capture patterns specific to protein families. Even with 65 million sequences, ESM can't learn all family-specific constraints—the training data is too sparse for rare families. MSAs provide targeted evolutionary data for the specific protein being predicted.
  • Structural epistasis: Mutations don't act independently—the effect of mutating residue i depends on the state of residue j. MSAs reveal these epistatic relationships through patterns of allowed/disallowed mutation combinations.
  • Quality and depth matter tremendously: A deep, high-quality MSA (thousands of sequences) provides more signal than a shallow MSA (tens of sequences). Language models provide the same embeddings regardless of MSA availability—they can't adapt to the specific evolutionary context.
Current Challenges with MSAs Despite their power, MSAs have significant limitations:
  • Computational bottleneck: MSA generation requires searching massive sequence databases (hours of CPU time), then running multiple sequence alignment algorithms (additional hours). This dominates AlphaFold2's runtime.
  • Unavailable for orphan proteins: Designed proteins, synthetic proteins, and proteins from poorly-sequenced organisms may have no homologs. MSAs are sparse or non-existent, crippling MSA-based prediction.
  • Quality varies dramatically: Shallow MSAs (10-50 sequences) provide weak signal. Deep MSAs (10,000+ sequences) provide strong signal. Prediction quality correlates strongly with MSA depth, creating a predictability gap.
  • Database growth is slowing: Sequence databases are growing, but not as fast as they once did. The "easy" genomes have been sequenced. Further growth requires expensive metagenomics projects.
  • Privacy and IP concerns: For therapeutic proteins or industrial enzymes, companies may not want to submit sequences to public databases to generate MSAs, limiting applicability.
Evidence from SimpleFold's Failure: Apple's SimpleFold attempted to replace MSA pathways with ESM embeddings and achieved worse performance than AlphaFold2. This suggests:
  • Current language models don't fully capture co-evolutionary constraints that MSAs provide
  • The Evoformer's explicit pair representations benefit from aligned sequences, not just sequence embeddings
  • Evolutionary pressure encodes structural information not inferrable from single sequences alone
  • MSA depth provides a gradient of information—deep MSAs enable better predictions—that single-sequence models can't replicate
However, this doesn't mean the quest is futile. ESM-2 is already remarkably good at structure prediction from sequence alone. Future language models trained on more data with better architectures might close the gap. The question is whether they can fully replace MSAs or only reduce dependence on them.

Optimal Path Forward: Hybrid Architectures Rather than abandoning either approach, the field is moving toward hybrid systems:
  1. Adaptive architecture: Use MSA processing when high-quality MSAs are available, fall back to language model embeddings when MSAs are sparse or unavailable. This provides graceful degradation rather than catastrophic failure.
  2. Phylogenetically-informed training: Rather than indiscriminate sequence collection (Evo2's failing), use directed evolutionary sampling focusing on specific clades with known evolutionary relationships. Yun Song's work on vertebrate/mammal-focused models shows this improves performance.
  3. MSA quality modeling: Explicitly model MSA quality and weight its contribution accordingly. A shallow MSA should contribute less to predictions than a deep MSA. Current models treat all MSAs equally regardless of depth.
  4. Synthetic MSA augmentation: Use generative models to create plausible evolutionary variants that enrich sparse MSAs. This could be particularly powerful for designed proteins or orphan proteins.
  5. Structure-aware language models: Pre-train language models on both sequence and structure, forcing them to learn what MSAs implicitly encode about structure. This could enable single-sequence models to partially replace MSAs.
  6. Uncertainty-aware predictions: Explicitly model uncertainty when MSAs are unavailable or low-quality. Rather than making overconfident wrong predictions, flag cases where more experimental validation is needed.
Lessons from SimpleFold: We're not yet ready to abandon MSAs. But the path forward isn't just collecting more sequences indiscriminately. Smarter use of evolutionary information—phylogenetic context, MSA quality weighting, hybrid architectures—shows more promise than simple scaling of sequence databases or model parameters.

Computational Resources and Prioritization

If computational resources were unlimited—access to vast TPU farms with essentially no cost constraints—what sequences or molecular entities would I prioritize for large-scale structure prediction or ensemble modeling?

My Strategic Thinking on Resource Prioritization I reasoned that I wouldn't start with DNA-protein binding interactions, despite their biological importance. DNA-binding proteins follow more consistent patterns—specific motifs mediate binding, and while they exhibit conformational changes, these tend to follow predictable patterns. They're "promiscuous" in that they can bind different DNA sequences, but there's an underlying regularity.

Instead, I'd prioritize systems with complex conformational dynamics where current methods struggle: MHC-peptide-TCR complexes because of their immunological importance and complex ternary interactions; transmembrane proteins like EGFR where conformational changes upon ligand binding trigger cellular signaling cascades; unique structural systems like bacterial flagella that contain rich information about protein assembly and conformational coordination; and intrinsically disordered proteins or multi-domain proteins with complex conformational landscapes.

The reasoning here is strategic: with unlimited compute, we should tackle the hardest problems that would maximally advance the field. DNA-binding proteins are relatively well-understood compared to transmembrane receptors, where we lack even basic structural information for many conformational states.

Rationale for System Prioritization
  • Transmembrane proteins as priority targets: These span lipid membranes and undergo dramatic conformational changes. EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) changes conformation upon ligand binding, triggering dimerization and activation of intracellular kinase domains. We have static structures of active and inactive states, but understanding the transition requires conformational ensemble modeling. These are major drug targets—most marketed drugs target GPCRs and other membrane proteins.
  • Multi-domain proteins with complex dynamics: Many proteins have multiple domains connected by flexible linkers. The relative orientation of domains is often functional—open vs. closed states regulate activity. AlphaFold2 struggles with these because the PDB is biased toward domain-domain interfaces (closed states), missing open states that may be transient but functionally important.
  • Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs): These lack fixed structure but play crucial roles in signaling, regulation, and cellular organization. AlphaFold2 gives them low confidence scores (appropriately), but provides no information about their conformational ensembles or how they become ordered upon binding partners.
  • Protein assemblies and complexes: Bacterial flagella, viral capsids, and other supramolecular assemblies show sophisticated conformational coordination. Understanding assembly pathways requires ensemble modeling of multiple proteins simultaneously.
  • MHC-TCR as exemplar of conformational complexity: The ternary complex of peptide-MHC-TCR involves three flexible components. The peptide can adopt multiple conformations in the MHC groove, the MHC itself has measurable flexibility, and TCRs show induced-fit binding. This system is functionally critical (immune recognition) and technically challenging (ternary complex, all components flexible).
Strategic Resource Allocation Philosophy With unlimited compute, the question becomes: what would maximally advance structural biology and human health? Simple answer: tackle the problems current methods can't solve.

Tier 1 Priorities (Highest Impact):
  1. pMHC-TCR complexes for immunotherapy: Understanding how T-cell receptors recognize peptide-MHC complexes would revolutionize vaccine design and cancer immunotherapy. We could design TCRs that specifically recognize tumor antigens, predict off-target toxicity, and understand autoimmune recognition. Direct path to clinical applications treating cancer and autoimmune diseases.
  2. Membrane protein conformational dynamics: GPCRs, ion channels, transporters, and receptor tyrosine kinases represent >50% of drug targets but are severely underrepresented in structural databases. Understanding their conformational dynamics would enable structure-based drug design for currently "undruggable" targets.
  3. Intrinsically disordered protein ensembles: IDRs are involved in signaling, transcriptional regulation, and liquid-liquid phase separation. Understanding their conformational ensembles could reveal new therapeutic approaches for cancer, neurodegeneration, and viral infection.
  4. Antibody-antigen recognition: Predicting how antibodies recognize antigens, including conformational changes upon binding, would accelerate therapeutic antibody development. Current methods struggle with antibody CDR loop prediction and induced-fit binding.
Tier 2 Priorities (High Scientific Value):
  1. Allosteric proteins and regulation: Understanding how signals propagate through proteins via conformational changes is fundamental to biology. This requires ensemble modeling to capture intermediate states and transition pathways.
  2. Enzyme catalytic mechanisms: Many enzymes undergo conformational changes during catalysis. Capturing these transition states would reveal catalytic mechanisms and enable enzyme engineering.
  3. Protein-protein interaction dynamics: Most proteins function in complexes, and many undergo conformational changes upon binding. Understanding these induced-fit mechanisms is key to predicting and designing protein interactions.
  4. Viral protein dynamics: Many viral proteins undergo dramatic conformational changes during infection—spike protein opening, viral fusion, capsid assembly. Understanding these could reveal intervention points.
Why Not Start with DNA-Binding Proteins? While important, DNA-binding proteins are relatively tractable. We understand their binding modes (helix-turn-helix, zinc fingers, etc.), and AlphaFold2 predicts them reasonably well. With unlimited compute, we should focus on harder problems where current methods fail.

Clinical vs. Academic Impact: When asked whether I'd prioritize differently for clinical impact, my answer was clear: the systems above (MHC-TCR, membrane proteins, IDRs) have both academic interest AND clinical impact. This is the sweet spot—fundamental biology that directly enables therapeutic applications.

Clinical Translation: From Models to Medicine

How do we translate computational advances into therapeutic impact? This is where my interest in MHC modeling connects to broader questions about using ensemble modeling for drug discovery and personalized medicine.

Clinical Priorities: pMHC-TCR and Cytokine Engineering When asked about prioritizing systems for clinical impact versus academic impact, I highlighted recent work from David Baker's lab that exemplifies the therapeutic potential of conformational modeling. They're pursuing two major directions: engineering T-cell receptors (TCRs) that bind specific peptide-MHC complexes for cancer immunotherapy, and developing pseudocytokines—designer proteins that mimic natural cytokine activity but with improved specificity.

Both systems absolutely require conformational dynamics understanding. TCRs must recognize the peptide-MHC complex, which involves induced-fit binding and conformational changes in all three components. Pseudocytokines must bind receptors and induce specific signaling cascades, requiring precise control of conformational changes that trigger downstream activation.

These systems funnel directly into the vaccine pipeline and personalized medicine. For cancer patients specifically, pMHC-TCR engineering enables CAR-T therapies and therapeutic TCRs targeting tumor neoantigens. The clinical promise is substantial and immediate.

Prioritized Clinical Systems

System 1: pMHC-TCR Complexes for Immunotherapy
  • Central to adaptive immunity: T-cells survey peptides displayed on MHC molecules. If they recognize tumor antigens, they kill cancer cells. This is how the immune system naturally fights cancer—immunotherapy just enhances this process.
  • Current therapeutic approaches: CAR-T cells use synthetic receptors targeting tumor surface proteins. Therapeutic TCRs use natural or engineered TCRs targeting intracellular tumor antigens displayed on MHC. Checkpoint inhibitors release brakes on T-cell activation. All benefit from better understanding of MHC-peptide-TCR recognition.
  • Why conformational modeling is essential: The pMHC-TCR complex is not rigid—all three components undergo conformational changes upon binding. The peptide can shift in the MHC groove, the MHC groove can adjust shape, and TCRs show dramatic CDR loop movements during binding. Static structures miss this completely.
  • Direct clinical applications:
    • Design TCRs that specifically recognize tumor neoantigens (mutated proteins in cancer cells)
    • Predict off-target toxicity where designed TCRs accidentally recognize self-peptides (prevents autoimmune side effects)
    • Understand why some tumor antigens are immunogenic while others aren't (guides vaccine target selection)
    • Engineer higher-affinity TCRs that more effectively kill cancer cells
    • Predict cross-reactivity where one TCR recognizes multiple related peptides (could be therapeutic or toxic depending on context)
  • Current limitations without ensemble modeling: We can't predict TCR-peptide-MHC binding accurately. Clinical trials of designed TCRs sometimes fail due to unexpected off-target recognition causing autoimmune toxicity. We can't computationally screen TCRs before expensive cell-based assays. Ensemble modeling could provide computational pre-screening, saving time and money in therapeutic development.
System 2: Cytokines and Pseudocytokines
  • Natural cytokines as therapeutic targets: Cytokines regulate immune responses—promoting inflammation or suppressing it depending on the cytokine. They're naturally used as therapeutics (interferon for hepatitis, IL-2 for cancer), but natural cytokines have problems: short half-life, systemic side effects, and pleiotropic effects (activating many pathways when you want one).
  • Baker lab's pseudocytokine approach: Design proteins that bind cytokine receptors and activate them, but with improved properties: longer half-life, reduced side effects, and pathway selectivity. This requires understanding conformational changes—how cytokine binding triggers receptor dimerization and conformational changes that activate intracellular signaling.
  • Why conformational modeling matters: Cytokine receptors undergo large conformational changes upon ligand binding. Understanding these changes enables design of agonists (activate receptor), partial agonists (weak activation), or antagonists (block activation). Fine-tuning the conformational change allows tuning the strength and duration of signaling.
  • Clinical applications:
    • Targeted immune modulation without systemic side effects
    • Pseudocytokines that activate only specific cell types (spatial control)
    • Biased agonists that activate beneficial pathways while avoiding toxic pathways
    • Long-lasting cytokine activity reducing administration frequency
    • Safer, more specific immune therapies for cancer, autoimmune diseases, and chronic infections
System 3: Transmembrane Receptors
  • EGFR and receptor tyrosine kinases: EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) is mutated in many cancers. Drugs like gefitinib and erlotinib target EGFR, but resistance inevitably develops through mutations. Understanding conformational dynamics could enable drugs targeting multiple EGFR conformations or predicting resistance mutations.
  • Conformational changes upon activation: EGFR exists in inactive monomers. Upon EGF binding, it undergoes conformational change enabling dimerization. Dimerization brings intracellular kinase domains together, activating signaling. Each step involves conformational changes—ligand binding, receptor dimerization, kinase activation.
  • Ion channels as drug targets: Many ion channels are drug targets (calcium channels for hypertension, sodium channels for pain, potassium channels for arrhythmia). These undergo dramatic conformational changes between closed, open, and inactivated states. Understanding these transitions enables state-specific drugs.
  • Clinical impact:
    • Design drugs targeting specific conformational states (inactive vs. active)
    • Predict and prevent resistance mutations that alter conformational dynamics
    • Allosteric drugs targeting non-active sites but altering conformational equilibrium
    • Expand druggable target space to currently intractable membrane proteins
Translation Challenge: From Computational Predictions to Clinical Trust Academic models optimize for benchmark performance—accuracy on test sets, speed, novelty. Clinical applications require entirely different priorities: reliability (consistent predictions), interpretability (understandable to clinicians), uncertainty quantification (knowing when predictions are unreliable), and validation against gold-standard experiments before clinical use.

The gap between these requirements is substantial. A model that's 90% accurate on benchmarks sounds good academically, but 10% error rate in clinical applications means some patients receive wrong treatments. This is unacceptable.

Clinical Requirements for Ensemble Models:
  • Calibrated uncertainty quantification: The model must know when it doesn't know. If predicting TCR binding to a peptide-MHC complex, it should flag cases where it's uncertain rather than making overconfident wrong predictions. Clinicians need to know which predictions to trust and which require experimental validation.
  • Mechanistic interpretability: Clinicians and regulatory agencies need to understand why a prediction was made. "The model predicts this TCR will bind this peptide because it forms these specific contacts and adopts this conformation" is acceptable. "The neural network gave a high score" is not acceptable for life-or-death decisions.
  • Robustness to input perturbations: Small changes in input (slightly different peptide sequence, minor differences in MHC allele) shouldn't cause dramatic prediction changes unless biologically justified. The model should be stable and predictable.
  • Experimental validation before deployment: No computational model should be trusted for clinical decisions without extensive experimental validation. The model should suggest candidates, experiments validate them, and only validated predictions inform clinical decisions.
  • Regulatory compliance: If computational predictions are used to select therapeutic targets (e.g., choosing which tumor neoantigens to target for vaccines), regulatory agencies may require understanding of model training, validation, and limitations. This demands documentation and interpretability far beyond academic standards.
Optimal Path to Clinic: Hybrid Human-AI Collaboration
  1. Interpretability first: Understand models mechanistically before deploying clinically. If we don't know what BioEMU learns about conformational dynamics, we can't trust it for therapeutic design.
  2. Uncertainty quantification at every step: Flag low-confidence predictions for experimental validation. High-confidence predictions can proceed faster; low-confidence predictions require more scrutiny.
  3. Hybrid workflows where models assist experts: Models don't replace human expertise—they augment it. Computational predictions screen millions of possibilities, narrowing to hundreds for expert review, which narrows to tens for experimental validation.
  4. Iterative validation and improvement: As clinical data accumulates (which TCRs actually worked in patients, which drugs succeeded), feed this back to improve models. This creates a virtuous cycle where clinical experience improves predictions.
  5. Regulatory frameworks for AI-assisted drug design: Work with regulatory agencies (FDA, EMA) to establish guidelines for using computational predictions in therapeutic development. This provides clarity for companies and safety for patients.
MHC-Based Cancer Vaccines as Ideal Test Case: Personalized cancer vaccines represent an ideal application for ensemble modeling:
  • High unmet medical need (cancer is deadly, current treatments limited)
  • Clear validation path (measure patient immune response to vaccine)
  • Direct clinical impact (if vaccine works, patients survive longer)
  • Relatively fast clinical timeline (cancer vaccines are being tested now)
  • Economic value justifies computational investment
If BioEMU-based ensemble modeling can improve neoantigen selection (identifying which tumor mutations to target for vaccines), improving vaccine response rates even modestly could save thousands of lives. This would establish ensemble modeling as clinically valuable, paving the way for broader adoption.

Data Quality vs. Quantity: The Evo2 Lesson

A recurring theme throughout my research and the discussion was the critical importance of data quality over raw quantity. This came into sharp focus when discussing recent controversies around large-scale genomic foundation models.

The Evo2 Data Quality Problem I raised concerns about the Evo2 model's claims of novelty in synthetic protein generation. The Evo2 team claimed their synthetically generated proteins had no identity to anything in the Protein Data Bank—suggesting genuinely novel designs. However, when researchers searched these sequences against genomic databases (rather than just PDB), they found approximately 80% identical homologs for proteins that were claimed to be novel.

This reveals two critical insights: First, the PDB has been exhausted as a sole training source—we've learned what we can from the ~200,000 experimental structures. Second, simply scaling to more genomic data doesn't solve fundamental challenges in protein modeling. Throwing more sequences at the problem without considering data quality, phylogenetic relationships, or functional annotation leads to diminishing returns.

Phylogenetically-Informed Models: The Better Path

Quality Over Quantity: Song's Approach I contrasted Evo2's approach with Yun Song's work from Berkeley, which uses phylogenetically-informed training. Rather than indiscriminately incorporating all available genomes (the "every genome and their mother's genome" approach), this strategy focuses on vertebrates and mammals with clear, well-understood evolutionary relationships.

A more directed, curated approach—understanding evolutionary context, ensuring phylogenetic balance, filtering for quality—appears substantially more useful than throwing massive computational resources at unstructured data. The benchmarks support this: Evo2 underperformed expectations despite its massive scale, while phylogenetically-informed models achieved better results with less data.

This observation led to one of my key insights during the discussion, which I stated plainly: "I don't think throwing GPUs at a problem is going to solve the problem." Computational resources are valuable, but they're not a substitute for careful thought about what data to use and how to use it.

Why Indiscriminate Scaling Fails
  • Genomic databases have systematic biases: Model organisms (E. coli, yeast, humans) are vastly overrepresented. Extremophiles, archaea, and environmental microbes are underrepresented. Training on all available sequences means the model learns mostly about model organisms.
  • Annotation quality varies dramatically: Many sequences in databases have uncertain or incorrect functional annotations. Some are computational predictions never validated experimentally. Some are fragments from metagenomic assemblies that may not represent real proteins. Training on low-quality annotations teaches the model noise.
  • Redundancy provides minimal new information: If 80% of your training sequences are 80%+ identical to existing sequences, you're not learning about new protein structures or functions—you're just oversampling the same structural classes.
  • Phylogenetic imbalance: Within-species duplication, whole-genome duplications, and gene family expansions create clusters of highly similar sequences. Without phylogenetic correction, these dominate training, while unique proteins from poorly-sequenced organisms are underweighted.
  • The "streetlight effect": We sequence what we can easily sequence (culturable organisms, model organisms, medically relevant organisms). This creates systematic gaps in our coverage of protein sequence space. Scaling doesn't fill these gaps—targeted sequencing of understudied organisms does.
Current Problem: Exponential Data Growth, Diminishing Returns Genomic databases are growing exponentially—millions of new sequences added each year from metagenomic projects, single-cell sequencing, and population genomics. But not all data is equally valuable for training structure prediction or ensemble generation models.

Evo2 exemplifies the scaling fallacy: assume more data always helps, collect everything available, train the biggest model possible. This approach achieved mediocre results despite massive computational investment. Why? Because data quality, not quantity, is the limiting factor.

Dimensions of Data Quality:
  • Phylogenetic context matters: A sequence from a novel bacterial phylum provides more new information than the 10,000th E. coli genome. But without phylogenetic weighting, all sequences contribute equally to training.
  • Annotation quality hierarchy: Experimentally validated function > computationally predicted function > unannotated sequence from metagenome. We should weight high-quality annotations more heavily or use them for validation rather than training.
  • Structural coverage gaps: Some protein folds are highly overrepresented in databases (immunoglobulins, kinases, GPCRs) while others are rare (novel folds from extremophiles). We need to sample sequence space to maximize structural diversity, not just sequence diversity.
  • Functional diversity vs. sequence diversity: Two proteins with 30% sequence identity might have identical function (homologs), while two proteins with 80% identity might have different functions (paralogs after sub-functionalization). Sequence identity alone doesn't capture functional diversity.
  • Distribution bias toward easily-studied proteins: Soluble, stable, easily-expressed proteins are overrepresented. Membrane proteins, toxic proteins, and proteins requiring specific conditions are underrepresented. Models learn what's easy to study, not what's functionally important.
Optimal Data Strategy: Curation Over Collection
  1. Phylogenetically balanced sampling: Ensure equal representation across major evolutionary clades. Don't let E. coli and its relatives dominate training just because we've sequenced thousands of E. coli strains.
  2. Quality-weighted training: Weight examples by annotation quality. Experimentally characterized proteins (PDB structures, validated functions) contribute more to training than computationally predicted proteins or unannotated metagenomic sequences.
  3. Diversity maximization: Actively select sequences that increase coverage of sequence/structure/function space. Cluster sequences and sample cluster representatives rather than including all members.
  4. Functional annotation integration: Incorporate what proteins do, not just what they look like. A sequence annotated with enzymatic activity, binding partners, and cellular localization provides richer training signal than a bare sequence.
  5. Active learning for targeted sequencing: Use model uncertainty to identify regions of sequence space where experimental characterization would maximally improve predictions. Then target those for sequencing and structural characterization.
  6. Synthetic data generation with constraints: Rather than training on low-quality real data, generate high-quality synthetic data using models constrained by physics and known protein principles. This may be better than massive-but-noisy real data.
Song's Success as Validation: The success of phylogenetically-informed models validates this approach. By focusing on vertebrates and mammals with well-understood evolutionary relationships, restricting training to high-quality genomes, and maintaining phylogenetic balance, these models achieve better performance than Evo2 despite using far less data and compute.

Lesson for the Field: The future of protein modeling isn't "biggest model trained on most data." It's "smartest curation strategy, highest quality data, best architectural inductive biases." We need to shift from the scaling paradigm (more is better) to the curation paradigm (better is better).

The Conformational Dynamics Revolution

The overarching lesson from both my research and the discussion is clear: the field is undergoing a shift from static structure prediction to conformational ensemble modeling. AlphaFold2 solved the static structure problem for most proteins. The next frontier is dynamics—understanding not just what proteins look like, but how they move, what conformational states they access, and how dynamics enables function.

BioEMU represents one approach to this challenge: learn from massive MD simulations to generate conformational ensembles. But it's not the only approach, and the field is exploring multiple paths:

  • Physics-based simulation at scale: Continue improving force fields and scaling MD simulations. Neural network potentials offer near-QM accuracy at MD speed.
  • Hybrid physics-ML approaches: Use ML to accelerate MD sampling (enhanced sampling methods guided by ML), or use MD to train ML models (BioEMU's approach).
  • Pure ML from experimental data: Train directly on experimental ensemble data (NMR, cryo-EM heterogeneity) without MD intermediary.
  • Coarse-grained models with ML: Combine coarse-graining (reduce degrees of freedom) with ML (learn effective potentials at coarse-grained level).

Each approach has strengths and weaknesses. The winning strategy may combine multiple approaches—physics-informed ML that learns from simulations but can also incorporate experimental constraints and adapt to new systems beyond the training distribution.

The Interpretability Imperative

  • We can't debug failures systematically: When BioEMU generates implausible ensembles, we can't diagnose whether it's a data problem, architecture problem, or training problem without interpretability.
  • We can't transfer insights across systems: If we don't know what features BioEMU uses for kinases, we can't know if those features apply to GPCRs or other protein families.
  • We can't build trust for clinical applications: Regulatory agencies and clinicians demand mechanistic understanding before trusting predictions for therapy design.
  • We can't improve models strategically: Without knowing what's missing from current models, we resort to blind architecture search rather than targeted improvements.

The opportunity is clear: become the first to systematically interpret an ensemble generation model. Establish methodology, build tools, and demonstrate value. This would not only advance BioEMU but establish interpretability as a core component of structural biology ML.

Clinical Translation Requires Different Standards

The discussion of clinical applications highlighted a crucial point: academic ML and clinical ML have fundamentally different requirements. Academic work optimizes for benchmark performance and novelty. Clinical work requires reliability, interpretability, uncertainty quantification, and extensive validation.

This gap is bridgeable but requires conscious effort:

  • Build uncertainty quantification from the start: Don't just predict—quantify confidence. Flag uncertain predictions for experimental validation.
  • Design for interpretability, not just performance: A model that's 95% accurate but interpretable may be more valuable clinically than 97% accurate but opaque.
  • Validate against gold standards, not just benchmarks: Clinical applications require validation against patient outcomes, not just benchmark metrics.
  • Document thoroughly: Regulatory compliance requires understanding training data, validation procedures, known limitations, and failure modes.
  • Collaborate with clinicians early: Don't develop a model in isolation then try to convince clinicians to use it. Involve them from the start to ensure clinical relevance.

MHC-based cancer vaccines represent an ideal test case for this translation pipeline. The need is urgent (cancer is deadly), the validation path is clear (measure immune response), and the timeline is relatively fast (vaccines are already in clinical trials). Success here would establish ensemble modeling as clinically valuable.

Data Quality Over Quantity: A Paradigm Shift

The Evo2 discussion crystallized a crucial insight: the field needs to shift from quantity-focused scaling to quality-focused curation. The era of "collect all available data and train the biggest model" is ending. Diminishing returns from indiscriminate scaling are becoming apparent.

The new paradigm prioritizes:

  • Phylogenetic balance over raw sequence count: Representative sampling across evolutionary diversity, not oversampling model organisms.
  • Quality-weighted training over equal weighting: High-quality experimental data contributes more than low-quality computational predictions.
  • Diversity maximization over redundancy: Sequences that expand coverage of protein space are more valuable than near-duplicates of existing sequences.
  • Multi-modal integration over single-modality scaling: Combining structural, dynamic, and functional data provides more signal than more sequences alone.
  • Active learning over passive collection: Strategically select what to characterize experimentally based on model uncertainty.

Song's success with phylogenetically-informed models validates this approach. Better curation beats bigger scale. This lesson applies beyond genomics to structural biology broadly: curate training data thoughtfully, weight by quality, and design for diversity.

Open Questions That Excite Me

Several questions emerged from the discussion that I'm eager to explore:

  1. Do ensemble models learn thermodynamics or just statistics? Can we probe BioEMU representations to see if it encodes free energy barriers, transition rates, and other thermodynamic quantities? Or does it just learn statistical patterns from MD trajectories without understanding the underlying physics?
  2. How do we optimally sample ensembles for different applications? Is two samples optimal for some purposes? Should we adapt sampling to prediction confidence? Can we develop application-specific sampling strategies?
  3. Can structure guide learning in low-data regimes? For rare MHC alleles or novel proteins, can geometric constraints from structures compensate for lack of training data? How do we optimally combine physics-based and data-driven approaches?
  4. What experimental data most improves ensemble predictions? If we could collect one type of additional data (cryo-EM, HDX-MS, NMR, SAXS), which would most improve BioEMU? How do we optimally integrate multi-modal experimental data?
  5. Can interpretability reveal design principles? If we understand what features BioEMU learns about conformational dynamics, can we extract design principles for protein engineering—rules for creating proteins with desired dynamic properties?
